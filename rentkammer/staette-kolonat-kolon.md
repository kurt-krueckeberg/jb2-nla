# Stätte, Kolon, and Kolonat

In the case files, the Krückebergs and their neighbours appear as the *Kolon*
(or *Colon*) of a numbered *Stätte*, such as Krückeberg No. 10 in Berenbusch.
This did not mean that they owned the house and land in the modern sense. The
land belonged to a lord, the *Grundherr*. For the holdings in this book that
lord was the ruler of Schaumburg-Lippe, and the Rentkammer managed his property
(see [What Was the Rentkammer?](what-was-the-rentkammer.md)). The Kolon held a
hereditary right to live on the holding and use it, and in return he owed dues
and labour services. He could not divide the holding, and he could sell it or
borrow against it only with the lord's consent. This is why so many of the case
files are petitions: the Kolon had to ask before he could change anything
attached to the Stätte.

## The terms

A **Stätte** was a recognized holding attached to one household and identified
by its house or holding number. It comprised the dwelling and whatever land,
buildings, rights and obligations were legally attached to it. A Stätte could
be a substantial farm, but it could also be little more than a house, a garden,
a few animals and a share in the common land.

A **Kolon** was the recognized holder of a Stätte. The word describes his
legal position, not his occupation. The Kolon of a small Stätte might earn most
of his living as a weaver, tailor, carpenter or day labourer.

The **Kolonat** was the tenure itself: the whole arrangement under which a
Kolon held a Stätte, with its rights, dues, services and need for the lord's
consent.

The **Grundherr** was the lord who owned the land. In Schaumburg-Lippe this
could be the ruler, a noble family, or a church institution. When it was the
ruler, the Rentkammer acted for him.

## Meierrecht: holding without owning

The legal form of this tenure in northwestern Germany was the **Meierrecht**.
The Grundherr owned the land, and the *Meier* (in Schaumburg-Lippe usually
called the Kolon) held the right to use it. Originally this was a lease for a
fixed term, but by the eighteenth century it had become a hereditary right to
use land belonging to someone else.[^wiki-erb] The right was recorded in a
**Meierbrief**, and the new holder paid the lord a fee, the *Weinkauf*, when it
was issued.[^wiki-brief]

The arrangement was much more secure than a modern lease. A lord could remove a
Kolon (*Abmeierung*) only for serious failings that endangered the holding,
such as neglect, bankruptcy or arrears of dues.[^wiki-abm] As long as the dues
were paid and the holding was kept up, the family could expect it to pass from
one generation to the next.

What the Kolon owed is listed in the 1849 sale of
[Krückeberg No. 10](../139/actum-bueckeburg-amt-21-aug-1849.md). The holding
consisted of a dwelling, a bakehouse and about 1¾ Morgen of garden land. It
owed 12 days of *Handburgfest* labour, 5 harvest days and 6 days of forest
work, money dues to the Amt, the land tax (*Contribution*), eggs and a small
payment to the parish of Petzen, a "smoke hen" (*Rauchhuhn*), a watchman's fee,
and the usual village and community duties.

The same case file shows how far the lord's consent reached. In 1824 the widow
Krückeberg had to [petition the Rentkammer](../139/doc1-1.md) to be relieved of
the holding's labour services. In 1849 Johann Heinrich Krückeberg had to ask
the Amt and the Rentkammer for permission to sell the Stätte before he
emigrated. The buyer took over the *Konsensschulden* attached to it: debts the
holding could carry only because the lord had consented to them. Without that
consent a Meier was not permitted to mortgage or pledge his holding.[^wiki-pfand]

If the heir was a minor, or the succession was unsettled, the holding could be
run for a time by an **Interimswirt** or **Interimskolon**, an acting holder
whom the lord had to accept.[^wiki-int] Archival descriptions in Bückeburg list
an *Interimskolon* and a Kolon for the same numbered Stätte, such as Hansing
and Schwabe at No. 12 in Großenheidorn.[^l101b]

## Anerbenrecht: one heir, no division

Under the **Anerbenrecht**, the Stätte passed whole to one heir, the **Anerbe**.
The aim was to keep the holding permanently undivided as one economic unit,
rather than splitting it among all the children until the pieces were too small
to support a household.[^wiki-anerbe] The heir was often the eldest son, but in
some places it was the youngest son, and a daughter could inherit if there were
no sons.[^wiki-anerbe2] A daughter who inherited was called an **Erbtochter**.
No single rule applied to every Stätte in Schaumburg-Lippe. The choice depended
on local custom, family arrangements and the lord's approval.

The other children were the **weichende Erben** ("yielding heirs"). They did
not receive land. They were paid out (*Abfindung*) from the family's cash and
from any freehold property it had, not from the Stätte itself.[^wiki-abf] A
settlement could take the form of money, a marriage portion, household goods,
or the right to stay on and work.

"Undivided" refers to the Stätte and the rights and duties attached to it, not
to everything the family owned. Movable property could still be shared out
separately.

Because a Stätte could not be divided, people who did not inherit one often
lived as **Einlieger** (lodgers) on someone else's holding. One of the few ways
for them to obtain a holding of their own was to be granted a new Stätte as a
*Neubauer*. The lodger Triftmeier received
[Neubauer-Stätte No. 19](../147/index.md) in Berenbusch in this way, and the
lodgers Vogt and Brüggemann received the new holdings
[Nos. 13 and 14 in Nordholz](../1291/index.md).

## Land held as property

Not every piece of land a Kolon used was held under the Meierrecht. In 1818 the
Rentkammer sold small plots of forest land to Krückeberg No. 10 and to the
Neubauer Brüggemann. Both purchase contracts say that, because the price had
been paid, "das Eigentum des erwähnten Platzes" (ownership of the said plot)
passed to the buyer and his heirs ([Krückeberg](../1291/doc19.md),
[Brüggemann](../1291/doc22.md)). Ownership did not free the land from charges.
The buyer and his heirs still had to pay a clearing rent (*Rottzins*), the land
tax, and the ordinary and extraordinary dues assessed on the plot.

A family could therefore hold its Stätte under the Meierrecht and at the same
time own land it had bought. When a case file speaks of a Kolon's land, it is
worth checking which of the two is meant.

## Names: the Hof- or Stättenname

In parts of northwestern Germany, including part of Schaumburg-Lippe, a family
could be identified by the name attached to its holding (the *Hofname*) rather
than by descent alone.[^whitton] When an Erbtochter inherited a Stätte and her
husband became its recognized holder, records might later call him by the
holding's name instead of, or together with, his birth surname.

The records are not consistent about this. A man may appear under his birth
surname, under the holding's name, under both joined by *genannt* ("called"),
or under different names at different times. A change of surname after a
marriage can therefore mean that a man married onto a Stätte, not that he was
born into the family whose name he now carried.
